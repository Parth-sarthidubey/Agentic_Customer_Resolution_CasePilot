CasePilot

An autonomous customer-resolution agent that investigates like an analyst, acts like an operator, and proves its work like an auditor.

The loop

observe → decide → act → verify → adapt

The test

A case closes only when SQL against the systems of record agrees with what the agent promised.

The proof

Stock sells out mid-plan. The agent re-plans, refunds, and verifies the money moved.

01 · Problem statement

Every support case looks simple.
Almost none of them are.

A customer writes: “my kettle arrived cracked — can you send a replacement by Tuesday? It’s a birthday gift.” No order number. No product code.

To resolve that one message a human must find the customer and order, check the policy window, work out whether a replacement can physically reach them by Tuesday and from which warehouse, choose between replacement, refund, credit or escalation, execute it in systems that can refuse — and confirm the money actually moved.

8–15 minutes

of skilled human work, per routine case, across five systems.

Hours to first reply

for an answer that could have been immediate.

Resists automation

because the right answer depends on facts nobody has gathered yet.

Rules engines
break the moment reality deviates — they cannot tell “stock exists” from “stock exists but arrives too late to matter”.
KB chatbots
can explain the refund policy but cannot issue the refund, so the work still lands on a human.
One LLM call
cannot verify itself, cannot recover when an action is refused, and will confidently promise a refund that never posted.
02 · Target users

Three people, three different needs

Support agents

Receive a diagnosed case with evidence gathered and the action already taken — or ready for one click — instead of a raw ticket and eight browser tabs.

Support leads

Keep control of the money. Approval thresholds, a queue of exactly what needs a human, and an audit trail of every action and its reason.

Customers

Get a resolution in seconds instead of a first reply in hours — and a straight explanation when the answer is no.

Built on a fictional home-goods retailer, Kestrel Home, with nine customers, twelve orders, three warehouses with real transit times, and a nine-policy handbook.

03 · Why this needs an agentic system

The path is discovered, not planned

Ambiguity
“one of my items arrived broken”, from a customer with two recent orders, is not actionable. The system must notice, ask one question, and resume.
Adversarial
stock sells out, carriers collect early, gateways return 503 — during the resolution.
Proof
“I issued the refund” is a claim. The refund in the payment ledger is a fact. Only the second closes a case.
Restraint
a fraud-flagged claim, or a refund above the limit, must stop. Not acting is part of acting correctly.

07 · Verification, guardrails, restraint

Done is a fact, not a claim

Verified in SQL

Seven checks against the systems of record, including a no_over_refund invariant on every case. A failed check re-enters planning — it is not a warning.

Guardrails in code

Refund > 150, credit > 50, or a fraud flag freezes the plan for a human. The model is never asked whether approval is needed, so no message can widen the limit.

Knowing when to stop

Two of eight demo cases end in not acting: an escalation with evidence, and a clarifying question. Both are correct outcomes.

Honest about the boundary

The enterprise systems are simulated — but simulated with real behaviour: constraints enforced, actions genuinely refused, state persisted. Swapping in real APIs touches sandbox/services.py alone; nothing in the agent layer assumes a simulation.

Built for weak models

Typed output contracts, an independent auditor, code-enforced guardrails, objective verification and bounded retries. Correctness does not rest on the model being clever — and a deterministic fallback completes every scenario with no API key at all.
